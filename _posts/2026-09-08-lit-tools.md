There is a question you ask constantly while writing a paper and can almost never
answer cheaply: *has someone already said this?* Not "is there literature on this
topic" — search engines answer that — but, for this particular sentence, who said
exactly it, who said a weaker version, who said a stronger one, and who said the
opposite. The honest answer usually requires reading forty papers you have already
downloaded and never opened.

`lit-tools` is a small plugin of three
[Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code) skills that automate the mechanical half
of that and refuse to automate the other half. This post documents how they work and,
more usefully, *why* they work that way: nearly every design decision in them is a
scar from a specific failure.

## The one design rule

**The tools know nothing about any subject. The corpus knows everything.**

Every subject-specific fact — which research traditions the literature spans, what
words each of them uses for the same idea, which filenames lie — lives in the corpus
directory, never in the plugin. Consequently one installation serves a corpus on
relational biology, one on algebraic topology and one on chemistry at the same time,
and none of them contaminates the others.

A corpus is just a directory:

```
<corpus>/lit-corpus.json         identity, layout, traditions, bibliography
<corpus>/texts/                  extracted full text, one file per source
<corpus>/texts-norm/             OCR-robust search mirror (generated)
<corpus>/index/bibmap.tsv        curated slug <-> bibkey <-> tradition
<corpus>/index/MANIFEST.tsv      generated: size, OCR quality, status
<corpus>/index/CORPUS-MAP.md     routing blocks — what to ask each source
<corpus>/index/glossary.md       the dialects each tradition uses for one idea
<corpus>/index/topic-terms.txt   weighted vocabulary for ranking candidates
<corpus>/index/TRAPS.md          filenames that lie, duplicates, bad scans
<corpus>/findings/               one file per claim searched
```

Scripts locate it by `--corpus PATH`, else `$LIT_CORPUS`, else by walking up from the
working directory to a `lit-corpus.json`. Two candidates side by side is an error,
never a guess.

## `lit-corpus-init` — a folder is to a corpus what a set is to a category

The entry point takes a folder of PDFs, a `.bib`, or both:

```bash
python3 init_corpus.py ~/corpora/relbio --name relbio --pdfs ~/Downloads/relbio
python3 init_corpus.py ./dump --bib refs.bib --paper-bib ../refs.bib --cited-from ../main.bbl
```

Each PDF is identified by the DOI embedded in it, falling back to a first-page title
lookup, so opaque publisher filenames (`1-s2.0-S0022519383712345-main.pdf`) are fine.
The two inputs contribute different things and it is worth being blunt about it: PDFs
contribute *text*, which is the thing that actually gets searched; a `.bib` contributes
clean keys, titles and years and **no text at all**. A corpus seeded from a bibliography
alone is mostly gaps, and the skill is instructed to say so out loud — "48 entries, 11
with text, 37 in `WANTED.md`" — rather than report a number that sounds like coverage.

The mechanical half is that one command. The judgement half is the point, and it is
four artefacts written by hand:

1. **Traditions.** Which communities does this corpus span, and what vocabulary marks
   each? The distinction that matters is *dialect*, not subject: a paper applying one
   field's machinery to another field's objects belongs to the tradition whose words it
   uses.
2. **A glossary**, one row per idea, one column per tradition, filled with what each
   community calls it. This is the highest-value file in the corpus, because claim
   search decomposes a claim across those rows before it searches anything. A dash where
   a tradition has no word for an idea is information, not a gap.
3. **Topic terms**, weighted, including *anti-terms* with negative weights for the
   adjacent literatures that would otherwise flood the expansion queue.
4. **Routing blocks**, one per source: what it establishes, what it does *not*
   establish, its dialect, what to route to it for.

Skipping step 3 is survivable. Skipping steps 1, 2 and 4 degrades the sibling skills in
ways that are invisible from the outside: routing silently falls back to hit counts, and
cross-dialect claims silently return nothing. A corpus with texts and no domain
knowledge is a folder.

## `lit-claim-search` — the actual question

Given a claim, the skill answers: who in the corpus said it, said less, said more, or
said the opposite. It runs in two phases.

### Phase 1: routing assets, deliberately cheap

There is **no up-front full read of the corpus.** Summarising a million tokens against
queries you have not yet seen compresses away the one sentence that will matter, and
once routing trusts that summary the miss is undetectable. So Phase 1 builds only a
manifest and a routing map from abstracts and section maps. Per-source cards, with a
claim inventory and a "does not prove" section, are written *lazily* — whenever a deep
read opens a source, that reading is written down before the context holding it is
discarded. Over time the sources the work actually leans on acquire detailed cards and
the rest cost nothing.

### Phase 2: decompose, route, fan out, verify, record

**Decompose.** Restate the claim as its formal content, then list the phrasings each
tradition would use for it. In a corpus worth searching this way the communities rarely
share a word, so this step does most of the work.

**Route.** Read the corpus map, then search — with `find.py`, never with `grep`:

```bash
python3 find.py "closure to efficient causation"
python3 find.py -C3 "noncomputable" "non computable"     # OR over patterns
python3 find.py -l -f Arbib "entailment"                 # counts only
```

The reason is OCR. Scanned journals come out letter-spaced (`a u t o m a t a`) or
word-joined, and notation is mangled beyond recognition — and those scans are precisely
the old papers on which a priority claim turns. `find.py` matches against a whitespace-
and punctuation-free mirror of every text, so `(M,R)-system` and `mr system` are the
same pattern and a phrase broken across a line break still matches. On one real test
query, `grep` found two of three files and missed the only relevant one.

Then rank the hits **by the map, not by hit count**. This is not fastidiousness. On a
real query, the corpus's foundational-but-irrelevant text scored 435 hits and the single
relevant source scored 1. Lexical scoring over a multi-tradition corpus does not merely
underperform; it inverts the truth. To the ranked list one adds two or three **cold
reads**: sources the map shows are topically adjacent but whose dialect differs enough
that no query of yours would ever reach them. That is where uncited priority hides, and
lexical search structurally cannot go there.

**Fan out — and never read the texts yourself.** One cheap subagent per candidate
source, four to eight in a single parallel batch, each given the claim, the
decomposition, its *one* file, and an output contract. The binding constraint on this
work is not capability but context: a single deep read of an OCR'd paper can cost more
context than the entire answer it feeds, and a full window degrades the session exactly
when the synthesis matters. The orchestrator sees only routing assets and hit lists.

**Verify.** Every reported hit is re-checked by searching a distinctive fragment of the
quoted text at the stated line, and any hit that fails is dropped. This output becomes
citations in published work; an invented quote is the one unrecoverable failure mode.

**Record.** The claim, its decomposition, the hit table, and the negative results go to
`findings/<claim-slug>.md`.

### The output contract

Every hit is a *relation to the claim*, never a similarity score:

| verdict | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `same` | priority citation — someone got there first |
| `weaker` | special case or extra hypotheses → cite as antecedent, say what you drop |
| `stronger` | ⚠️ the claim may be subsumed by existing work |
| `assumed` | asserted without proof → proving it is the contribution |
| `contradicts` | must be addressed in the text |
| `adjacent` | cite as context, not as priority |

Each hit carries a bibkey, a `slug:Lstart-Lend` reference, a verbatim quote, a verdict,
one line of rationale, and a paste-ready citation line. And every search closes with
three sections that are worth as much as the hits: **searched, nothing found**, per
source — which is the evidence for a novelty claim; **not in corpus**, listing stubs and
failed extractions, so a null result can never imply coverage that does not exist; and a
**confidence** note, lowered for sources with poor OCR.

## `lit-expand` — growing along the citation graph

The third skill adds sources rather than searching them, in the same formats, so
anything ingested is immediately searchable.

**Discovery is structural, not lexical.** Candidates come from the citation graph of the
sources already present — what they cite, and what cites them — because a corpus worth
building this way spans traditions with disjoint vocabulary for the same ideas. A
keyword sweep both misses papers that word things differently and drowns in papers
sharing only surface terms. A citation edge to a seed is far stronger evidence of
belonging than any phrase. For a focused subject the sweep is near-exhaustive: seeds
have citing sets of tens to a few hundred works, not thousands.

Four stages, each writing a durable artefact, each resumable:

```bash
python3 resolve_seeds.py       # corpus     -> index/SEEDS.tsv
python3 harvest.py             # SEEDS      -> index/candidates.jsonl
python3 rank.py                # candidates -> index/CANDIDATES.md   <- REVIEW GATE
python3 ingest.py --from-queue # marked rows -> texts/ + bibmap + bibliography
python3 wanted.py              # stubs      -> index/WANTED.md
```

Resolution is a ladder — DOI, then OpenAlex title search, then trimmed title, then
**Crossref's fuzzy bibliographic query**, then give up. Crossref is not a nicety:
publisher digitisations of old journals mangle deposited titles (one 1966 paper is
deposited with its notation replaced by lookalike characters), so no title query against
OpenAlex can ever reach the papers such a corpus is built on. Nothing below a 0.80
title-similarity cutoff is accepted; an early version took the top hit and filed one
author's 1982 paper under another's slug.

Ranking scores a candidate as `4 × (distinct seeds connected) + vocabulary weight`.
Degree dominates by construction, for the reason given above, and it still surfaces an
OCR-mangled paper with no abstract at all — exactly the case keyword search cannot
reach. Tier **A** is degree ≥ 3 (or ≥ 2 with strong vocabulary), **B** is degree ≥ 2,
**C** is a single edge kept on vocabulary alone. The split matters at scale: on one full
frontier of 1006 works, 343 had an open-access PDF but 312 of those were tier C — one
citation edge plus a topical word. Ingesting them would have quintupled the corpus with
material one weak link from the subject. Fetch A and B in bulk; treat C as a reading
list, not a queue.

Then `rank.py` **stops.** Nothing is ingested until a human marks the queue: `[x]` full
text, `[s]` stub with metadata only, `[ ]` skip. Reading a long queue can be handed to a
batch of cheap subagents, but the marking cannot be delegated, because a bad screening
call does not announce itself — it becomes a corpus entry that later gets cited in
published work.

Acquisition is another ladder, because an open-access link is usually not a PDF: in one
bulk run 36 of 51 "OA" URLs failed, fourteen of them bare `doi.org` links resolving to
HTML landing pages. The ladder tries arXiv, then Europe PMC, then the given URL, then
the `citation_pdf_url` meta tag on the HTML it got back; that recovered nine more.
Elsevier and MDPI block programmatic fetches outright and are not recoverable at all —
which is what `WANTED.md` is for. It lists paywalled papers ordered by citation degree,
and when you obtain them through institutional access, one command ingests the lot,
matching each PDF to its stub by the DOI inside it:

```bash
python3 ingest.py --promote-dir ~/Downloads
```

## Four invariants

Stripped of implementation, the whole thing rests on four rules, each of which exists
because violating it produced a wrong answer that looked right.

**Never `grep` an OCR'd corpus.** A scan can render a word letter-spaced or joined to
its neighbour; the match has to happen against a normalised mirror.

**Rank by citation degree, not keywords.** Over a multi-tradition literature, lexical
scoring inverts the truth — 435 hits for the irrelevant classic, 1 for the paper you
needed.

**A rate limit is not an absence.** Every pipeline stage stops on a 429 and keeps what
it has. Catching the exception and recording "not found" once demoted 25 correctly
resolved seeds, silently.

**A paper with no reachable text is a stub, never a silent drop.** Claim search reports
stubs as *unsearched*, so a null result cannot masquerade as novelty. This is the same
principle as the review gate: the system is allowed to be ignorant, but never allowed to
present ignorance as knowledge.

That last one is really the thesis of the whole plugin. The expensive part of a
literature search is not finding things; it is knowing precisely what you failed to
look at.
